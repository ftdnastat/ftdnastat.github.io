How we searched

Two sources: FTDNA geo-project members on 1° cells (a family votes once) and FTDNA customers by country. For every child branch with at least 5 carriers in 3 places we found the spot with the most of them within 400 km. The other branches count as close when three quarters of them sit within 800 km of their centre; the branch that left is 1,500 km or more away and has 10 carriers or more. Carriers of one sub-branch at one place were shuffled between the branches as a whole, up to 50,000 times: a split is listed when such a gap almost never came out by chance (Benjamini–Hochberg, 5 %). The common ancestor's place is Discover's migration-path point, and our own model from the same customers where Discover has none.

STR ages against SNP ages #

A branch's age is how long ago the common ancestor of all its members lived. There are two ways to estimate it. SNPs are rare mutations, and Discover dates branches by them. STRs are the fast-changing markers in ordinary tests, and we date branches by them ourselves: the farther the descendants' haplotypes have drifted apart, the older the branch. By STR, branches usually come out 1.9 times younger than by SNP, and the older the branch, the wider the gap. Below are the branches where the two methods disagree most.

How it is counted

An STR age works like this: the farther the haplotypes have drifted from the most common (modal) one, the older the common ancestor. For 5,323 branches with 20 or more distinct 37-marker haplotypes (124,998 kits of open projects; identical haplotypes count once) that age is set against Discover's SNP estimate; 30 years a generation, the spread comes from random resamples of the haplotypes. STR to SNP (below one means STR is younger): median ×0.53, 19% of the branches land within a factor of 1.5. By SNP age band: under 1,000 years ×0.64 (41%, 253 branches); 1–3 thousand ×0.59 (31%, 2,042 branches); 3–10 thousand ×0.51 (12%, 2,571 branches); over 10 thousand ×0.29 (0%, 457 branches). The older the branch, the more STR understates it: the marker spread stops growing with time, so the lists below hold only branches under 10,000 years by SNP. On 67 markers (4,698 branches) the median is ×0.52.

We take samples with the 37-marker panel from the tables of open FTDNA projects and from our collection. Each sample goes to the topmost branch of its line younger than 1,000 years by Discover, so it counts once. A branch needs at least 10 distinct haplotypes: 447 branches, about 8,818 samples per marker. A difference is a sample's marker off the branch's most frequent value; a jump is 2 steps or more off, and for markers with several copies also copies that became equal while the branch's differ (RecLOH). We take the share of jumps among differences. Two-step moves are set against an ordinary marker, the median of single-copy markers; equal copies have nothing to compare with, so they are shown apart. A fast marker has more steps and more jumps alike, so the share does not depend on the mutation rate.

How we searched

We take FTDNA branches with at least 20 subbranches: 8,360 branches, 5,378 of them with ancient samples at the parent. For a branch we count Discover ancient samples and living carriers over its whole subtree and divide by the number of subbranches, then compare with the same for the parent. So that a small branch does not stand out by chance, we add 20 subbranches at the parent's level. The thick-by-ancient lists take branches with at least 5 such samples, the thin ones those where the parent's level would give at least 5. A branch whose kin is already listed is not repeated. On the scatter a grid step means twice; the living-carrier scale is stretched and extreme values are pinned to the edge. Before a branch is its breadcrumb trail from the letter's root: the thicker the line under a name, the further that branch stands from its parent. For mtDNA (mitochondrial DNA) the same on FTDNA's public mt tree: 269 branches, 266 of them with ancient samples at the parent; ancient samples come from Discover's Mitotree, each placed on a public branch by its haplogroup's name or by the pair of a renamed branch.
