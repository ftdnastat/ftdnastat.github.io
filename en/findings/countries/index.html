Paternal and maternal lines: are they linked, and how much of it is just geography #

Both lines are known for one person, and they are not independent: knowing the paternal line lets you guess something about the maternal one. The link was measured over 11,261 YFull pairs with a known country (41 countries with 80+ pairs). Knowing only a person's country explains 1% of the whole link between their lines. Take only the pairs with a known region (2,532 pairs, 27 regions with 50+ pairs). On them the country explains 42% of the link, the region 21%.

Fathers tell the people apart, mothers do not #

Do paternal and maternal lines paint the same picture of a people? For each people we drew 40 samples at random and measured the diversity of their major branches (Shannon entropy), for Y and for mt separately. Percentages compare that mix with the world's in FTDNA data: 100% is the world's, more is more mixed than the world. Of 15 peoples the most uniform by mothers keeps 89% of the world's mix, the most uniform by fathers only 35%: by mothers, peoples can hardly be told apart.

How we searched

We take YFull peoples with at least 50 Y samples mapped to major branches of the FTDNA tree: 19 peoples, 135 pairs from different language families by Glottolog. A people's profile is the shares of major branches, similarity is one minus the Jensen–Shannon distance between branch shares, as for similar countries. A people sits where most of its samples are, region or country. Over all cross-family pairs we fit similarity against distance: the usual similarity that far apart. Then we redraw each people's samples from itself 200 times and count how often the pair comes out no closer than the line; Benjamini–Hochberg at 5% corrects for testing many pairs. A pair must lie below the line by more than pairs usually stray from it, and one people gives at most 2 pairs. Small samples scatter more, so they pass less often. A shared branch shows its share in the first and in the second people.
